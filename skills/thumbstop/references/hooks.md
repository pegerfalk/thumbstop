# Hooks

The first 1.5 s decide whether anyone hears the rest. Every hook lands its layers **together at t = 0**: the first frame, the hook
line, and a sound. Without a voice the hook line is the big text on frame 0;
with `--voice` it is spoken and a short screen text adds to it. None of them is a logo or an introduction.

Official backing (verified 2026-09-23, `platforms.md`): TikTok says
"Introduce your content proposition in the first 3 seconds"; Meta says "nail
the hook within the first few seconds". Thumbstop's 1.5 s is deliberately
stricter.

## Rules for every hook

- **The payoff is in the video.** A hook promises exactly what the video
  delivers. No bait.
- **The first frame is not blank, dark or a title card.** It is the product,
  a motion already under way, or a contrast.
- **With `--voice`, voice and screen text differ.** The screen text is the
  keyword or the number (≤ 5 words); the voice says the sentence. Without a
  voice, the line itself is the screen text: short enough to read in one
  glance (2–7 words).
- **True.** A number or claim in a hook needs a provenance row like any other.
- **The number flexes.** Pick the biggest true figure (the shop's total, not
  one item's) and say what it counts (`step-2-script.md` § 5).
- **Specific.** If the hook would work for any product, rewrite it.

## The seven types

The examples use one made-up project, a studio selling hand-thrown mugs, so
the types are easy to compare. Real hook seeds, with sources, belong in the
project profile.

### 1. Question — `question`
Ask the thing the viewer is now wondering about what they see.
- Frame: a mug on the wheel, mid-throw, clay still wet.
- Line: *"Why does a handmade mug cost four times a shop one?"*
- Screen: `4× the price?`

### 2. Bold claim — `bold-claim`
A short statement that sounds too big until the video proves it.
- Frame: a mug dropped onto a wooden floor, not breaking.
- Line: *"This mug will outlive your kettle."*
- Screen: `outlives the kettle`

### 3. Number — `number`
A concrete, sourced figure up front. Numbers stop thumbs better than adjectives.
Use the biggest total that fits the claim (every mug the studio has sold, not
the sales for one glaze), with the unit written out (mugs, orders, buyers) and
rounded down with a "+" (`1,200+ mugs`). The picture shows the whole range
when the number is the studio's.
- Frame: a row of finished mugs, one lit.
- Line: *"Nine days from lump of clay to your shelf."*
- Screen: `9 days`

### 4. Before / after — `before-after`
Show both states inside the first second, or the after first.
- Frame: flash-forward: the glazed mug for 0.7 s, then the grey lump of clay.
- Line: *"Same clay. Nine days later."*
- Screen: `same clay`

### 5. "You're doing X wrong" — `doing-it-wrong`
A gentle correction of a habit the viewer has. Never mocking.
- Frame: a mug going into a dishwasher.
- Line: *"Your favourite mug is losing its glaze in there."*
- Screen: `in there?`

### 6. Curiosity gap — `curiosity-gap`
Open a loop the viewer needs closed; close it at the payoff, not before.
- Frame: a macro of a glaze pattern, no mug visible yet.
- Line: *"No two of these come out of the kiln the same."*
- Screen: `why?`

### 7. Visual pattern break — `visual-break`
The motion is the hook; the line is minimal.
- Frame: the kiln door opening on glowing shelves.
- Line: *"Watch the colour change."*
- Screen: `watch the colour`

## Choosing

Step 2 writes three options from at least two types and lets the user pick.
Defaults per format are in `formats.md`. Once videos are live, compare
retention per hook type and re-order this list; until then, the order above
means nothing.
